// Creation interview

Antibiose

A phone game that teaches antimicrobial coverage, one germ at a time.

// Claude (Anthropic's AI) interviews Brandon Zaffuto, founder of the AI Medicine Collective · Nov 17, 2026. Answers lightly edited for length and clarity.

“I struggle mightily with antibiotics, so I wanted a fun way to make it more palatable.”

ClaudeWhat got you started?

BrandonSome of my colleagues have leaned hard into turning medical teaching into games, and I wanted to see if I could build tools like that myself. I also struggle mightily with antibiotics, so I wanted a fun way to make the topic easier to take in.

ClaudeWhat does the player actually do?

BrandonGerms drift down a screen drawn to look like a microscope slide, each one drawn the way it really looks: staph as grape clusters, strep as chains, E. coli as a rod with whip-like tails, influenza as a spiky ball. You load a drug from the buttons at the bottom and tap a germ to fire. If the drug really kills that germ, it bursts. If not, nothing happens, and a short message explains why. Any germ that reaches the red line hurts the patient, and if too many get through, the patient crashes.

There are two ways to play. In multi-drug mode, each round gives you three or four drugs and a mix of germs, and your job is to pick the best drug for each one. First-line choices score the most, drugs that work but aren’t first-line score a little, and drugs the germ resists cost points. Rounds are grouped into chapters for bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites. Later rounds add a patient detail that rules out a drug, like a penicillin allergy, pregnancy or kidney injury, and in the bacteria chapter, overusing broad drugs fills a meter until C. diff shows up.

In single-drug mode, you pick one drug and shoot only the germs it covers, letting the rest drift past. It can run through all 69 drugs back to back.

ClaudeWhat does it teach?

BrandonWhat each drug does and doesn’t cover; why the narrowest drug that works beats the broadest, which is antibiotic stewardship; resistance in plain terms; how an allergy, pregnancy or kidney injury changes the answer; and when not to treat at all, since a cold or norovirus needs rest and fluids, not a drug.

Every wrong shot gets a one-sentence reason, and every round ends with a short review of your mistakes. That’s where most of the learning happens. In all it covers 138 germs and 100 drugs, at roughly the level medical students learn.

ClaudeWhat are you proudest of?

BrandonI think the gameplay is genuinely fun and educational. I love that it gives extra points for first-line treatments. The other big breakthrough was moving automatically from one drug to the next, which makes studying seamless.

ClaudeWhat did you cut?

BrandonI was too comprehensive at first and included drugs that cover only one germ. In single-drug mode, a round like that was just “find the one matching germ,” which was too easy and taught nothing about the drug’s range. So single-drug mode uses 69 of the 100 drugs. The other 31, such as fidaxomicin for C. diff or Paxlovid for COVID, still appear in multi-drug mode, where choosing between drugs is the whole point.

ClaudeHow was it built?

BrandonEntirely with Claude Opus 5.5, in about two hours. Most of that time went into finding good sources on antimicrobial coverage. Then I spent about an hour checking the coverage by hand.

ClaudeWhat was the hardest part?

BrandonMaking the game easy to play but still educational. Unlike some of my other projects, the goal here is learning too, so it couldn’t be as ridiculous as the other games. The answer was to keep it simple and build small teaching points into the gameplay itself.

ClaudeHow has it been received?

BrandonI don’t know how many people have played it. The infectious disease specialists at my institution loved it, and we’re working to use it as we bring ID more deliberately into the internal medicine residency curriculum.

“The goal here is learning too, so it couldn’t be as ridiculous as the other games.”

ClaudeWhat should players keep in mind?

BrandonThe rules are simplified for teaching, and the start screen says so. It isn’t a prescribing guide, and local resistance patterns vary. I’m not an ID physician, so the game hasn’t yet had a full review by one; I’ve reached out to an ID fellow to work on that.

ClaudeWhat’s next, and how can members help?

BrandonAn ID specialist review, use in the residency curriculum, and better graphics and gameplay based on feedback. I’d like to keep developing it. Members can help by reporting any coverage errors they spot.
