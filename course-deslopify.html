Editing AI writing, from draft to yours

AI can draft a patient message, an abstract or a personal statement in seconds. Left as it comes, the draft is often slop: padded, vague, oddly upbeat, and sometimes wrong. Readers notice, and in medicine they stop trusting what they read. This course shows you how to spot slop and fix it.

1 What slop is, and why it matters in medicine

Slop is text that sounds finished but says little. It is not the same as "written by AI": plenty of AI drafts are fine, and people wrote slop long before chatbots. What AI changed is the volume. You will see five signs again and again:

  • Padding. Openers, recaps, and sentences that repeat the one before.
  • Stock phrases. Words that turn up far more in AI text than in people's writing: delve, crucial, landscape, navigate, foster, testament.
  • Tics of shape. Everything in threes, "not just X, but Y", bullets and bold where a sentence would do.
  • Unearned confidence. Numbers, doses and citations that nobody checked.
  • No author. It could have been written by anyone, for anyone.

Why it matters here. Patients may act on a message you send. Program directors read hundreds of personal statements and recognize the pattern within a line. Editors and reviewers stop trusting a paper after one invented reference. Your name is on the text, not the model's.

    2 Cut the padding

    The fastest fix is deletion. AI drafts wrap the useful middle in politeness and recap. Read the draft once and strike:

    • Openers: "Great question!", "I hope this finds you well", "I wanted to take a moment to...".
    • Announcements: "It's important to note that", "It's worth mentioning", "Let's take a closer look". If it is important, the sentence shows it.
    • Recaps: "In summary", "In conclusion", and any last paragraph that repeats the first.
    • Stacked hedges: "may potentially help to some extent". Keep one hedge, the true one.
    • Formulaic closers: "Please don't hesitate to reach out if you have any questions or concerns."

    A good test: if you delete a sentence and nothing is lost, it should stay deleted. Most AI drafts shrink by a third without losing a fact.

        4 Fix the shape

        AI drafts have a recognizable rhythm even when every word is fine. Look for:

        • Automatic threes. "Clear, concise and compassionate." Ask whether there are really three things, or one.
        • The contrast slogan. "It's not just X, it's Y." "Not only... but also." Usually cut, or say Y plainly.
        • Cheerleading. "Let's dive in!", "You've got this!" Out of place in clinical writing.
        • Bullets and bold everywhere. Three short sentences read better than three bullets with bold labels. Keep lists for steps people follow in order.
        • Even rhythm. Every sentence the same length. Mix short and long.

          // A cleaner version

          Use your controller inhaler every day, even when you feel well. Keep your rescue inhaler with you. Call us if you need it more often than usual.

          5 Check every claim

          This is the lesson that matters most in medicine. Language models write the most likely next words, not checked facts. They can produce a confident number, a guideline that was revised, or a real-looking reference to a paper that does not exist or says something else.

          • Every number: open the source it came from. If there is no source, it goes.
          • Every citation: find the paper and read the part being cited. Real authors and journals are often attached to the wrong claim.
          • Every dose and every guideline: check the current guideline or a drug reference, never the draft.
          • Every "all", "always" and "never": guidelines rarely say them.
          • Reassurance: "nothing to worry about" is a clinical judgment. Make it yourself or remove it.

          And one rule before you start: never paste patient-identifying information into an AI tool that your institution has not approved for it.

          // Exercise 5 · A teaching slide on sepsis

          Tap the claims you would have to check before using this. (There are 3.)

          Sepsis causes about 11 million deaths worldwide each year (Rudd et al., 2020). Giving antibiotics within one hour cuts sepsis mortality by 50%. Guidelines recommend a 30 mL/kg fluid bolus for all patients with sepsis. Our hospital's bundle compliance last quarter was 64%.

            // What about the last sentence?

            It is your own hospital's figure, so you can check it at the source: the quality report it came from. The AI cannot know it; if a draft contains a local number you did not give it, it was made up.

            6 Make it yours, and ask for less slop

            The last pass is about the reader. A patient handout and a grant abstract need different words. For patients, aim for a 6th to 8th grade reading level: short sentences, everyday words, one instruction per sentence. Then add what only you know (the real follow-up date, the detail from your own experience) and read it aloud once. Anything you would not say out loud, change.

            Ask for less slop up front. A clear request saves editing. For example:

            Write this for a patient at a 6th grade reading level. Use only the facts I give you below. No greeting, no sign-off, no summary. Short sentences. If something is missing, ask me instead of guessing.

              // Keep this one

              The deslopify checklist

              Before you send anything AI helped you write:

              1. No patient-identifying information went into the AI tool.
              2. Cut the opener, the recap and the stock closer.
              3. Delete any sentence that repeats the one before.
              4. Swap stock phrases for plain, specific words.
              5. Undo automatic threes, contrast slogans, and bullets that should be sentences.
              6. Check every number, dose, guideline and citation against its source.
              7. Remove anything you cannot stand behind, including reassurance.
              8. Add what only you know: the real detail, date and next step.
              9. Match the reader. For patients, short sentences and everyday words.
              10. Read it aloud once.
