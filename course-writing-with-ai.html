Writing with AI, and staying the author

AI can help you think, draft and edit faster. It can also put words, facts and references in your mouth that you never checked. This course covers what journals, funders and residency applications generally allow, ways of working that keep the thinking yours, and how to take the AI out of a draft so it reads like you wrote it, because you did.

1 What is generally accepted

Journals, funders and application services word their rules differently, but they agree on more than you might expect. Five principles cover most situations:

  • You are the author, and you are responsible. AI cannot be listed as an author, because it cannot take responsibility for the work. Whatever it wrote, you answer for every word, number and reference.
  • Disclose when the venue asks. Medical journals following the ICMJE recommendations ask you to say at submission whether you used AI and how, in the cover letter and in the relevant part of the manuscript. Failing to disclose can be treated as misconduct.
  • AI is not a source. Never cite a chatbot for a fact. Find the primary source and cite that.
  • Keep confidential things out of AI tools. Patient information, a manuscript you are peer reviewing, a grant you are scoring, a colleague's unpublished data. NIH, for example, prohibits its peer reviewers from using generative AI on applications and treats uploading them as a breach of confidentiality.
  • The ideas must be yours. Help with brainstorming, wording and proofreading is widely accepted. Handing over the thinking is not: NIH will not consider applications substantially developed by AI to be the applicant's original ideas.

Where the rules come from. The details differ by venue, so always read the specific instructions too:

  • A journal manuscript. AI is not an author; disclose use at submission; you check everything. Some journals add stricter rules, for example on AI-made images. (ICMJE)
  • A peer review. The manuscript is confidential: do not upload it to an AI tool unless the journal says you may.
  • An NIH grant. Applications substantially developed by AI are not considered original; NIH says it uses AI detection. Reviewers may not use generative AI at all.
  • A residency or fellowship application. The AAMC accepts AI for brainstorming, proofreading or editing the personal statement, but the final submission should be your own work. (See lesson 5.)
  • Patient messages and handouts. Only tools your institution has approved for patient information; you sign it, so you check it.

    2 A way of working that keeps you the author

    The most useful habit is simple: think first, then bring in the AI. If the model writes before you have decided what you want to say, its ideas quietly become yours, and they are usually the most average ideas available. A workflow that works for papers, abstracts, letters and statements:

    1. Your raw material. Notes, data, the point you want to make, the story only you can tell. Messy is fine.
    2. Your outline. Three to six lines: what the reader should know, in order.
    3. AI as a critic. Paste your outline or rough draft and ask what is missing, unclear or unsupported. Decide yourself which points to take.
    4. Your draft. Write it, or rewrite any AI wording until it says exactly what you mean.
    5. AI as a copy editor. Ask for a list of suggested edits, not a rewrite, so you accept each one on purpose.
    6. Your checks. Every fact, number and reference against its source (lesson 4), then a read aloud.
    7. Your disclosure, wherever the venue asks for one.

    A quick test of whether you are still the author: could you defend every sentence to a reviewer, and explain where each claim came from?

      3 Prompts that help, and what never goes in one

      Good prompts give the model a narrow job, the facts it may use, and the reader. Some that work well in medicine:

      Critic. Here is my draft. Do not rewrite it. List the five places a skeptical reviewer would push back, and why.
      Reverse outline. Summarise each paragraph of my draft in one line. Then tell me where the order or the logic breaks.
      Copy editor. Suggest edits for grammar, clarity and length as a numbered list with the reason for each. Keep my voice; do not add claims.
      Plain language. Rewrite this for a patient at a 6th grade reading level. Use only the facts below. If something is missing, ask me instead of guessing.
      Other side. Give the strongest argument against my conclusion, using only the results I have pasted.

      What never goes in a prompt, unless the tool is approved by your institution for exactly that: anything that could identify a patient (names, dates, record numbers, addresses, photos, rare details), confidential manuscripts and grants you are reviewing, and anyone else's unpublished work.

      Tap what should not be in this prompt. (There are 4.) Every detail is made up.

      Help me write a discharge summary for Maria Lopez, MRN 00481927, born 03/14/1957, admitted with community-acquired pneumonia and treated with ceftriaxone and azithromycin. She is the only female harbour pilot in the county. Write it at an 8th grade reading level and use only these facts.

        // A safer prompt

        Help me write discharge instructions for an adult treated for community-acquired pneumonia with ceftriaxone and azithromycin. Write it at an 8th grade reading level and use only these facts. (And for real patients, use only the tools your institution has approved.)

        4 Check what the AI gives you

        Language models predict likely words; they do not look things up unless the tool says it did, and even then they can misread what they found. In medical writing the failures cluster in four places:

        • References. Real-looking citations to papers that do not exist, or real papers attached to claims they do not make. Open every one and read the part you cite.
        • Numbers. Effect sizes, percentages and sample sizes with no source. If you cannot find it, delete it.
        • Guidelines and doses. Old versions, or recommendations stated more broadly than the guideline does. Check the current document.
        • Your own details. If the draft mentions a date, a place or a result you did not give it, it was invented.

        Keep a two-column habit while you check: the claim, and where you confirmed it. It takes ten minutes and is the best protection your name has.

          "Burnout is common among residents" is general enough to stand, though a reviewer may still want a citation. "Our program introduced monthly coaching in July" is your own fact, so you can confirm it yourself.

          5 Personal statements and applications

          What is allowed. The AAMC, which runs ERAS, accepts AI for brainstorming, proofreading or editing the personal statement, but the final submission should represent your own work. Other application services and individual programs may set their own rules, so read each one.

          Who reads it. The AAMC says the ERAS platform itself does not use AI to analyse, sort or evaluate applications. Some programs use other tools: the AAMC describes Thalamus Cortex, a review tool, as using AI to read personal statements and assess certain applicant characteristics, while not scoring, filtering or rejecting applicants. And then there are the faculty who read hundreds of statements a season.

          Detectors are not a safe bet either way. In a study of seven AI detectors, essays by non-native English speakers were wrongly flagged as AI-written 61% of the time on average. Do not count on going undetected, and do not assume a flag is proof. The only reliable position is a statement you could talk through, line by line, at your interview.

          How AI helps here.

          • Ask it to critique your draft: where is it vague, where does a claim lack a story behind it?
          • Use it as a copy editor and take its edits one by one.
          • If English is not your first language, use it to say clearly what you mean, and keep the facts and stories yours.
          • Have a mentor, not the AI, do the final read.

          The test of a good statement: no one else could have written it. AI's default sentences are the ones anyone could have written.

          // Exercise 5 · A personal statement paragraph

          Tap the sentences anyone could have written. (There are 3.)

          I have always been passionate about helping others and making a difference in people's lives. On my sub-internship, a man with heart failure asked me to explain his pills back to him every morning, and by Friday he was explaining them to me. This experience taught me the importance of patient education and empathy. Internal medicine offers the perfect blend of intellectual challenge and meaningful patient relationships.

            // What to keep

            The second sentence is the statement. Build the paragraph around it: what you did differently on Monday, what he taught you, what you still do because of it.

            6 De-AI your writing

            What de-AI means here. Not hiding AI use: where a venue asks you to disclose, disclose. It means editing a draft until it is accurate, specific and in your voice, so it reads like a person who knows the subject wrote it. A short pass:

            1. Cut the padding: openers, announcements ("It's important to note"), recaps and stock closers.
            2. Swap the stock words (delve, crucial, landscape, navigate, foster, testament, robust) for plain or specific ones.
            3. Break the shape: automatic threes, "not just X, but Y", bullets that should be sentences, every sentence the same length.
            4. Add what only you know: the real detail, number, date or moment.
            5. Take out what you cannot stand behind, including hedges you do not mean and confidence you have not earned.
            6. Read it aloud. Change anything you would not say.

            For a full drill on each step, take the Deslopify course.

            Dear Editor, I am thrilled to submit our manuscript for your consideration. In this study, we delve into how night-float handoffs affect overnight pages.               // A cleaner version

              Dear Editor, we submit a study of how night-float handoffs affect overnight pages. A structured handoff cut overnight pages by a fifth across 14 weeks, which we think will interest readers who run residency programs. We used an AI tool to edit the text for grammar, as described in the manuscript.

              // Keep this one

              Before you submit anything AI helped with

              1. I read this venue's rules on AI (journal, funder, application service, program).
              2. No patient-identifying or confidential material went into an AI tool that was not approved for it.
              3. The ideas, structure and conclusions are mine; I could defend every sentence.
              4. Every reference is a source I opened, and it says what I cite it for.
              5. Every number, dose and guideline is checked against its source.
              6. Nothing in it is a detail I did not supply myself.
              7. AI is not listed as an author or cited as a source.
              8. I disclosed AI use wherever the venue asks.
              9. I took out the padding, stock words and automatic shapes, and added what only I know.
              10. A person I trust read it, and I read it aloud.
